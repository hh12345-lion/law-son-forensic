Lost profits damages can be a significant issue in commercial litigation involving alleged breaches of contract, business disruption, failed transactions, and other commercial disputes. When a business claims that another party's conduct caused it to lose profits, the financial analysis may require an estimate of the results the business would reasonably have expected under different circumstances.

For attorneys, reviewing a lost profits damages model involves more than checking the arithmetic. The analysis may depend on projected revenue, profit margins, costs that would have been incurred, the period of alleged loss, and other factors that could have affected the business's performance.

A structured review considers whether the model is supported by financial records, whether its assumptions are explained, and whether the calculations address the circumstances of the dispute.

This article outlines key considerations for attorneys assessing a lost profits damages model in commercial litigation.

## What Is a Lost Profits Damages Model?

A lost profits damages model estimates the profits a business might have earned if an alleged wrongful act or other relevant event had not occurred. It generally compares an estimated financial outcome under that alternative scenario with the business's actual financial performance, subject to the methodology and legal framework applicable to the claim.

This is often described as a "but-for" analysis because it considers what the business's financial performance might have been but for the event in question.

The appropriate method depends on the facts, the type of claim, the available evidence, and applicable legal requirements. Lost revenue should not automatically be treated as lost profit because the business may have avoided some costs associated with generating that revenue.

For attorneys reviewing a damages claim, important questions include:

- What financial outcome does the model assume would have occurred?
- What evidence supports that alternative scenario?
- How does the model account for actual financial performance?
- Which costs and other financial effects have been considered?
- How has the expert addressed uncertainty and alternative explanations for the alleged loss?

These questions can help identify the assumptions that warrant closer examination.

## 1. Examine the Basis for Projected Revenue

Projected revenue is often a significant input in a lost profits calculation. The reliability of the estimate may depend on historical trading results, customer relationships, contractual commitments, sales forecasts, market conditions, and other business-specific evidence.

An attorney reviewing the model may ask:

- What historical period was used to develop the revenue forecast?
- Were sales trends consistent throughout that period?
- Were there significant changes in customer demand, pricing, or product mix?
- Were projected sales supported by contracts, purchase orders, or other contemporaneous records?
- Did the business have sufficient capacity to fulfil the projected demand?
- Were known market or operational changes reflected in the forecast?

Historical results can provide a useful starting point, but they do not necessarily establish what would have happened in the absence of the alleged event.

For example, a business may have experienced strong revenue growth before a disputed contract was terminated. A damages model might use that trend to estimate future sales. The analysis may warrant further examination if it does not adequately address changes in demand, customer concentration, competitive conditions, or other factors that could have influenced future performance.

### Compare Projections With Contemporaneous Evidence

Budgets, internal forecasts, customer correspondence, contracts, sales reports, and board materials may help explain what the business expected before the alleged loss event.

A forecast prepared before a dispute arose may provide useful context, although its timing alone does not establish its reliability. Its assumptions, preparation, and consistency with other evidence remain relevant.

Where the model relies on management estimates or hypothetical sales, the supporting rationale should be identifiable and open to examination.

## 2. Review the Profit Margin Applied to Lost Revenue

Revenue projections alone do not establish the amount of lost profits. The calculation also needs to consider the costs associated with generating the projected revenue.

Depending on the business and methodology, these costs may include:

- Materials and inventory
- Direct labor
- Sales commissions
- Shipping and distribution
- Transaction-specific subcontracting costs
- Other expenses that vary with sales or production

If the business did not incur certain costs because projected sales did not take place, those avoided costs might need to be reflected in the calculation.

For example, a company claiming lost revenue from cancelled orders may have avoided the materials and shipping costs associated with fulfilling those orders. Treating the full value of the cancelled orders as lost profit could overstate the financial loss, depending on the facts and the applicable measure of damages.

### Distinguish Fixed, Variable, and Mixed Costs

Cost behaviour can vary by business and over time. Some costs change with sales volume, while others remain relatively stable over a particular period. Certain expenses may contain both fixed and variable components.

An attorney may ask:

- How were the relevant costs classified?
- What accounting records support the classification?
- Were cost ratios consistent across the periods examined?
- Were changes in staffing, production capacity, or supplier arrangements considered?
- Does the model explain how avoided costs were calculated?

The analysis should reflect the circumstances of the business rather than assume that every expense falls neatly into a fixed or variable category.

## 3. Assess Expected Performance Against Actual Results

A lost profits model generally needs to explain how the projected financial outcome compares with the business's actual performance.

The comparison may involve revenue, gross profit, operating expenses, contribution margins, or other financial measures, depending on the methodology used.

Attorneys may consider whether the model:

- Identifies the relevant period of alleged loss
- Reconciles actual financial results with accounting records
- Separates the projected outcome from actual performance
- Accounts for revenue earned from substitute customers or alternative transactions where relevant
- Explains significant differences between forecast and actual results
- Addresses the possibility of counting the same financial loss more than once

Actual results can help test a model's assumptions, but they must be interpreted in context. A decline in revenue after a disputed event does not, by itself, establish that the entire decline was caused by that event.

Similarly, a business may have continued generating revenue despite the alleged disruption. The model may need to distinguish between lost opportunities and business activity that continued or was replaced.

## 4. Examine Causation and Alternative Explanations

A financial damages model should explain the relationship between the alleged event and the loss being quantified. Whether the legal requirements for causation are satisfied is ultimately a matter for the relevant legal process, but financial analysis can help examine the extent to which the claimed loss is supported by the available evidence.

Other circumstances may have affected the business during the claimed loss period, including:

- General economic conditions
- Changes in customer demand
- Increased competition
- Existing operational difficulties
- Supply chain disruption
- Pricing changes
- Loss of other customers or contracts
- Management or capacity constraints

An attorney may ask how the expert identified and considered these factors.

For example, if a business's sales declined during a period of wider market contraction, the analysis may need to examine whether and how that market condition affected projected results. The relevance of the issue will depend on the evidence and the method used to quantify the alleged loss.

The objective is not to assume that an alternative explanation necessarily defeats the claim. It is to understand whether the model distinguishes the effects attributed to the disputed event from other factors that may have influenced financial performance.

## 5. Review the Claimed Loss Period

The duration of the claimed loss can materially affect the total amount calculated. A damages model may estimate losses over a period that has already elapsed, a future period, or a combination of both.

Attorneys may consider:

- How was the start date selected?
- What evidence supports the end date?
- Was the business expected to resume normal operations?
- Were replacement customers or alternative arrangements available?
- Did the relevant contract, market, or operating circumstances change during the period?
- Are future losses based on assumptions that can be explained and tested?

The appropriate period depends on the facts and applicable legal requirements. It should not be selected solely because it produces a particular damages figure.

Where the model includes future lost profits, the underlying projections and treatment of future uncertainty may require additional attention.

## 6. Evaluate Mitigation and Replacement Business

Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, a lost profits analysis may need to consider efforts to reduce the loss and the financial effects of replacement business.

Mitigation may involve obtaining alternative customers, finding substitute suppliers, reallocating production, using available capacity, or changing business operations.

Relevant questions include:

- What steps did the business take after the alleged event?
- Were alternative customers or transactions pursued?
- Did replacement work generate revenue during the claimed loss period?
- Were additional costs incurred to secure substitute business?
- How were the financial effects of those actions reflected in the model?
- Does the analysis distinguish between revenue replaced and revenue genuinely lost?

The existence of mitigation efforts does not automatically determine the amount of recoverable damages. Their significance depends on the evidence and applicable legal principles.

From a financial perspective, the model should explain how relevant replacement revenue, additional costs, and other benefits have been treated so that the calculation can be assessed.

## 7. Check the Supporting Financial Records

A damages calculation is easier to evaluate when its principal inputs can be traced to identifiable source material.

Depending on the dispute, relevant records may include:

- General ledgers and trial balances
- Historical financial statements
- Tax returns
- Bank statements
- Sales reports and customer records
- Contracts, purchase orders, and invoices
- Payroll and staffing records
- Budgets and management forecasts
- Production and capacity reports
- Records of replacement transactions and additional expenses

The documents required will depend on the nature of the claim and the complexity of the business.

Attorneys may ask whether the expert reconciled key figures with the underlying accounting records and explained any material gaps, inconsistencies, or adjustments.

Where estimates are necessary because complete records are unavailable, the model should identify the assumptions used and explain their basis. Missing records do not automatically establish that a claim is unsupported, but their absence may affect the assessment of particular inputs.

## 8. Test the Model's Sensitivity to Key Assumptions

Some damages models rely on assumptions that can materially influence the final calculation. These may include projected growth rates, profit margins, customer retention, production capacity, the duration of the loss, or the treatment of certain expenses.

Sensitivity analysis examines how the result changes when selected assumptions are varied.

For attorneys, this can help identify which inputs have the greatest effect on the claimed amount and where further evidence may be useful.

Questions may include:

- Which assumptions have the largest effect on the result?
- How does the calculation change if projected revenue is lower?
- What happens if the assumed profit margin changes?
- How sensitive is the result to the claimed loss period?
- Are alternative scenarios consistent with the available evidence?
- Has the expert explained why the selected assumptions were used?

Sensitivity analysis does not establish which scenario is correct. It can, however, make the relationship between assumptions and the calculated amount more transparent.

## 9. Review the Expert's Methodology and Explanation

A lost profits damages model should be understandable to its intended audience. An attorney should be able to identify the principal inputs, understand the calculation process, and follow the reasoning connecting the evidence to the expert's conclusions.

A review may consider whether the expert:

- Identified the methodology used and explained why it was selected
- Explained the relevant financial assumptions
- Distinguished source data from estimates
- Addressed material limitations in the available records
- Explained adjustments made to historical results
- Considered relevant alternative explanations
- Presented calculations that can be followed and checked
- Identified the principal uncertainties affecting the conclusion

The appropriate methodology depends on the facts of the matter. A before-and-after analysis, comparison with a suitable benchmark, or another approach may be relevant in different circumstances. No single method should be treated as automatically appropriate for every dispute.

The important consideration is whether the method and its application are adequately explained and supported for the particular assignment.

## Questions Attorneys Can Ask When Reviewing a Lost Profits Claim

Before a deposition, expert meeting, mediation, or other litigation milestone, attorneys may find it useful to organise their review around the following questions:

1. What financial outcome does the model assume would have occurred absent the alleged event?
2. What historical records and other evidence support the projected revenue?
3. How were profit margins and avoided costs determined?
4. How does the model account for actual financial performance?
5. Were other factors affecting the business considered?
6. What evidence supports the claimed loss period?
7. How were replacement revenue and mitigation-related issues addressed?
8. Can the principal inputs be traced to source documents?
9. Which assumptions have the greatest effect on the calculated amount?
10. What limitations or uncertainties remain in the analysis?

The answers may help counsel identify areas requiring clarification, additional records, or further expert analysis. They do not, on their own, determine whether a claim is legally recoverable or what amount a court or other decision-maker may accept.

## Conclusion

Assessing a lost profits damages model requires attention to the relationship between the alleged event, the business's expected financial performance, its actual results, and the assumptions used to calculate the claimed loss.

Revenue projections, profit margins, avoided costs, causation, the loss period, mitigation, source records, and methodological choices may all warrant examination, depending on the circumstances.

For attorneys handling commercial litigation, a structured review can help identify the financial questions that need to be addressed and clarify how the expert reached the proposed damages figure.
